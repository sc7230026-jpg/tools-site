Mastering Your 401(k) Strategy

What Is a 401(k)?

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan that allows workers to save and invest a portion of their paycheck before taxes are taken out. Taxes aren't paid until the money is withdrawn from the account. It is one of the most powerful wealth-building tools available to employees due to its high contribution limits, tax advantages, and the potential for employer matching.

How a 401(k) Works

When you enroll in a 401(k), you decide what percentage of your salary you want to contribute. This money is automatically deducted from your paycheck and invested in a portfolio of mutual funds, index funds, or target-date funds of your choosing.

The funds grow tax-deferred. You cannot withdraw the funds without a 10% penalty until you reach age 59½ (with some exceptions). In 2026, the maximum employee contribution is $23,500, with a $7,500 catch-up contribution allowed for those aged 50 and older.

The Power of Employer Matching

An employer match is essentially "free money." If an employer offers a 50% match on up to 6% of your salary, and you earn $100,000, contributing $6,000 will result in your employer adding another $3,000 to your account. Always contribute at least enough to capture the full match. Failing to do so is leaving part of your compensation on the table.

Traditional vs Roth 401(k)

Traditional 401(k): Contributions are made with pre-tax dollars, lowering your taxable income today. Your money grows tax-deferred, and you pay ordinary income tax on withdrawals in retirement.

Roth 401(k): Contributions are made with after-tax dollars. You get no tax deduction today, but your money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are 100% tax-free. Roth is often mathematically superior for young workers or those who expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement.

How Inflation Impacts Retirement Savings

Inflation erodes the purchasing power of money over time. Historically averaging around 2.5% to 3% annually, inflation means that $1,000,000 in 30 years will buy significantly less than $1,000,000 today. Our calculator specifically provides an "Inflation-Adjusted Balance" to show you the real future purchasing power of your savings in today's dollars.
